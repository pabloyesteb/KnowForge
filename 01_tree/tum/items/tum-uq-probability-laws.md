# Measurable random variables, laws and expectations

A probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal A,P)\) consists of outcomes, a sigma-algebra of measurable events and a countably additive measure with total mass one. Completeness means every subset of a null measurable set is measurable; the notes assume completion. Almost-sure statements hold outside a null set. For a topological target, its Borel sigma-algebra is generated by open sets.

A random variable \(X:(\Omega,\mathcal A)\to(\Psi,\mathcal G)\) is measurable: inverse images of measurable target sets lie in \(\mathcal A\). Its realization is a value \(X(\omega)\), and its law is the pushforward \(P_X(G)=P(X^{-1}(G))\). The law is a probability measure on the target, not a density by definition. If the scalar law is absolutely continuous with density \(p_X\), then \(P_X(B)=\int_Bp_X(x)\,dx\); not every law has such a density.

For an integrable random variable the expectation is \(\int_\Omega X\,dP\). Changing variables by the law gives \(E[f(X)]=\int f(x)\,dP_X(x)\) whenever the integral is defined. Thus indicators recover event probabilities, and scalar expectations become \(\int xp_X(x)\,dx\) when a density exists. For Banach-valued simple functions \(s=\sum_jz_j\mathbf1_{A_j}\), the integral is \(\sum_jz_jP(A_j)\); norm-integrable approximation by simple functions defines the Bochner integral. A limit in the target Banach norm is essential, rather than pointwise convergence alone.
