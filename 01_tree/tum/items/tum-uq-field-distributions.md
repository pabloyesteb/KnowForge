# Random fields, consistent finite-dimensional laws and Gaussian specification

A real random field on \(D\subset\mathbb R^d\) is a family \(Z(x):\Omega\to\mathbb R\) of measurable variables. Fixing the outcome gives a realization (a sample path when \(d=1\)). The map \(\omega\mapsto Z(\cdot,\omega)\) is measurable into \(\mathbb R^D\) with its cylindrical sigma algebra, generated by finite point evaluations. Pointwise measurability alone does not supply joint spatial/probabilistic measurability or continuous paths.

The Daniell–Kolmogorov extension theorem constructs a field from finite-dimensional probability measures that are invariant under coordinate permutations and consistent under marginalization. The resulting law is unique on the cylindrical sigma algebra; this assertion concerns distributions, not equality of realizations on a chosen probability space.

A Gaussian field has jointly Gaussian vectors \((Z(x_1),\ldots,Z(x_n))\) for every finite collection of points. A real mean function and a symmetric positive semidefinite covariance kernel define consistent Gaussian finite-dimensional laws and hence such a field. These two functions uniquely determine its cylindrical law, including singular finite-dimensional distributions through characteristic functions. For general second-order fields, mean and covariance do not determine the full law.

For example, \(Z(t)=\xi_1\cos t+\xi_2\sin t\) with independent standard Gaussians is a centered Gaussian process with covariance \(\cos(s-t)\), by linearity of every finite-dimensional projection. Correlating the two coefficients with covariance \(\rho\) gives \(\cos(s-t)+\rho\sin(s+t)\); the coefficient covariance is admissible only when \(|\rho|\le1\).
