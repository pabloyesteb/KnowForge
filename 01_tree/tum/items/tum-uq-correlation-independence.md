# Joint laws, covariance and independence

The joint law \(P_{X,Y}\) is the law of the vector \((X,Y)\); expectations such as \(E[XY]\) use that law rather than the two marginal laws separately. For square-integrable scalar variables,
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y].
\]
Correlation divides covariance by the two standard deviations and is defined only when both are positive. Zero covariance means uncorrelatedness.

The sigma-algebra generated by \(X\) consists of inverse images of target measurable sets and is the smallest one making \(X\) measurable. Independence of two variables means independence of their generated sigma-algebras: probabilities of all corresponding event intersections factor. If a joint density exists, this is equivalent to \(p_{X,Y}=p_Xp_Y\) almost everywhere. Independent integrable variables have integrable product and factorized expectation, hence zero covariance when covariance is defined. The notes' first-moment hypothesis is enough for that product statement; the usual square-integrability assumption also ensures both variances exist.

Uncorrelated variables need not be independent. For \(X\sim N(0,1)\), put \(Y=\cos X\). The integrand \(x\cos(x)e^{-x^2/2}\) is odd, giving covariance zero, but the nonconstant \(Y\) is determined by \(X\). The Gaussian exception requires the pair to be jointly Gaussian, not merely Gaussian marginal distributions. For families, the notes define only pairwise independence in Definition 2.35; mutual independence requires factorization for every finite collection and does not follow from pairs alone.
